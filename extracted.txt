The Water You Can See Is Only 30 to 40% of the Problem
Mopping the puddle feels like progress. But in a typical Plano leak, 60% or more of the water has already moved where towels and box fans can't reach.
Under the floor:
 water slips past baseboards and gets trapped under LVP foam, hardwood adhesive or carpet pad. Nothing evaporates through vinyl or finished wood, so it sits on the slab and sours.
Up the walls:

The 4 Rules of Drying (Psychrometrics, Made Simple)
1. Airflow Strips the Wet Layer Off Every Surface
Wet drywall and wood are wrapped in a thin layer of saturated air. High-velocity air movers sweep it away and replace it with dry air, so the material keeps releasing water.
2. Dry Air Pulls Water Out of Wood
Water moves from wet to dry. Low-grain refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers dry the room air so far that moisture is pulled out of studs and subfloor into the air, then condensed and pumped out. They keep working at humidity levels where store-bought dehumidifiers freeze up.
3. Warmth Speeds Everything Up
A drying room held around 75 to 85°F pushes moisture out of dense materials like oak floors and concrete faster.
4. Some Materials Need Special Tools
Carpet and drywall respond to standard drying. Hardwood, plaster, brick and multi-layer subfloors hold "bound" water and need mats, wall injection or desiccant dehumidifiers. See
hardwood floor drying
 and
wall cavity drying
.

What does "grains per pound" mean on my drying log?
It's the weight of water vapor in a pound of air (7,000 grains equal one pound of water). When dehumidifier exhaust reads 15 to 25 grains lower than the room air, the machine is actively removing water.

Dehumidifiers give off heat as they pull water from the air. A room between 75 and 85°F actually dries faster.
